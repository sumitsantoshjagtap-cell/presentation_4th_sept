Opening

When you last searched Google — were you doing research?

Most people say yes. The technically accurate answer: probably not.

What we do instinctively

Information retrieval

Finding existing answers to an immediate question. Fast, convenient, built for consumption — not for generating or verifying new knowledge.

What research actually is

Systematic knowledge generation

A structured process of inquiry designed to answer questions that don’t already have clear, verified answers — and to produce conclusions others can scrutinize and build upon.

The distinction isn’t about effort or intelligence. It is about method, accountability, and intent. That is what we unpack today.

Definition

What is Research?

Research is a systematic, evidence-based, methodological process of inquiry aimed at generating, verifying, or extending knowledge on a specific problem or question.

Systematic

Follows a defined sequence of steps — not random exploration or instinct-driven browsing.

Evidence-based

Conclusions are grounded in data and verifiable sources, not opinion, assumptions, or anecdote.

Methodological

Uses established, discipline-appropriate procedures that others can examine, critique, and replicate.

Replicable

Another researcher following the same process should arrive at comparable findings under the same conditions.

Taxonomy

Types of Research

Research is categorized along three independent axes. A study can be applied + qualitative + secondary simultaneously — these are not mutually exclusive.

By Approach — Qualitative

Meaning, context, lived experience

Data: interviews, observations, texts. Asks how and why. Produces interpretive insights rather than statistical measures.

By Approach — Quantitative

Measurement, numbers, relationships

Data: surveys, experiments. Asks how much, how many, what correlation. Produces statistically analyzable results.

By Purpose — Basic

Expanding theoretical knowledge

Driven by curiosity about underlying principles. No immediate practical application required — the goal is knowledge itself.

By Purpose — Applied

Solving specific practical problems

Uses existing knowledge to address real-world needs. Most undergraduate projects fall into this category.

By Data Source — Primary

First-hand data collection

You collect the data yourself: surveys, interviews, experiments, field observations. Direct, original, yours.

By Data Source — Secondary

Using data collected by others

Published studies, government databases, historical records. Essential for literature reviews and most UG projects.

Relevance

Why Does Research Matter for You?

Regardless of your discipline, research literacy is a foundational academic and professional competency — not optional at the undergraduate level.

  • ✓
    Develops critical thinkingEvaluating claims, distinguishing evidence from opinion, and resisting misinformation — all trained by research methodology.
  • ✓
    Required for academic workDissertations, capstone projects, and final-year work all demand research skills. The earlier you build them, the better you execute later.
  • ✓
    Professional expectation across fieldsEmployers in business, policy, healthcare, technology, and education value evidence-based reasoning — not just in academia.
  • ✓
    You become a producer, not just a consumerEven a well-executed undergraduate study adds to what is known. Research skills separate someone who reads knowledge from someone who generates it.

Literature Review

What a Literature Review is Not

Clear the misconceptions first — they are the most common reason students produce a flawed review.

  • ✗
    Not a list of paper summaries“Paper 1 says X. Paper 2 says Y.” — This is annotation. A literature review synthesizes across papers, not through them.
  • ✗
    Not an annotated bibliographyAn annotated bibliography describes each source individually. A literature review draws thematic connections across sources.
  • ✗
    Not a Google search with citations bolted onFinding sources is the starting point of a literature review — not the review itself.
  • ✗
    Not a single opening paragraph“Several researchers have studied this topic...” is a sentence, not a review. A literature review is a substantive analytical section of your work.
  • ✗
    Not reading everything ever published on a topicA literature review is strategically scoped and purposively selected — not exhaustive consumption without synthesis.
Literature Review

What a Literature Review Actually Is

Definition

A critical, synthesized analysis of existing scholarship on a topic — written to map what is known, identify what is contested, and reveal what is missing; thereby establishing the scholarly context and justification for your own research.

Critical

You evaluate sources — assess their methodology, strength of evidence, potential biases, and limitations. You are not just a reporter of what others said.

Synthesized

You connect, compare, and organize what multiple sources say around themes and arguments — not paper by paper, but by idea and pattern.

Purposive

Every source you select serves the purpose of building toward your gap, argument, and research objective. Inclusion is deliberate, not coincidental.

The one sentence to remember: You are not reporting what others said. You are joining a scholarly conversation — and locating where your research fits within it.

Literature Review — Why Do It?

Three Functional Roles of a Literature Review

A literature review serves three distinct purposes. Think of them as three legs of a stool — without all three, the structure collapses.

Function 1

Find the Research Gap

Exposes where knowledge is absent, incomplete, contradictory, or contextually limited. This is what justifies the existence of your study. Without a gap, you have no rationale.

Function 2

Finalize Your Topic

You may think your topic is original. The literature review tells you whether it already has an answer, whether it needs to be narrowed, or whether your framing requires rethinking entirely.

Function 3

Support Your Research Facts

Grounds every significant claim in established scholarship. You do not build arguments from assertion — you build them from evidence that has already survived peer review.

A literature review that only performs one or two of these functions is incomplete. All three must be present for the review to genuinely serve your research.

Literature Review — Research Gap

What is a Research Gap?

Definition

A research gap is an absence, inconsistency, or under-examined angle within established literature — a question that existing research raises but has not adequately addressed, within a specific context, using appropriate methods, for a relevant population.

Most Common Misconception

“Nobody has studied this” is rarely a gap — it usually means you haven’t searched thoroughly enough. Research gaps are found within existing research, through the contradictions, silences, and limitations it reveals.

Empirical Gap

Limited or insufficient data and evidence on the topic. More or better data is needed.

Methodological Gap

Existing studies use methods that are inadequate, biased, or too narrow for the question being asked.

Context / Population Gap

Studied elsewhere (e.g. Western settings) but not in your specific context, region, or population.

Theoretical Gap

No existing framework adequately explains an observed phenomenon — a conceptual void in the field.

Literature Review — Types

Types of Literature Reviews

Different research goals call for different types. For most UG students, the narrative review is the relevant starting point.

Narrative Review — Most common at undergraduate level

Broad, thematic synthesis without a rigid pre-specified protocol

Organizes and synthesizes literature around themes the researcher identifies as relevant. Driven by analytical judgment, not a fixed protocol.

Best for: Understanding a topic broadly, mapping arguments, identifying themes and patterns.
Limitation: Source selection can introduce bias if not conducted thoughtfully and documented clearly.

This is the type most UG students will write. The standard is: thematic organization, critical analysis, and synthesis — not paper-by-paper description.

Systematic Review — Standard in evidence-based disciplines

Rigorous, protocol-driven, transparent, and reproducible

Follows a pre-registered, explicit protocol with defined keywords, databases, and inclusion/exclusion criteria. Results are fully reproducible by another researcher.

Best for: Clinical questions, policy decisions, evidence synthesis where rigor is critical.
Common in: Medicine, public health, psychology, evidence-based education.

UG students rarely conduct a full systematic review, but understanding its standards raises the bar for any LR you produce.

Scoping Review — Mapping the landscape of a field

Identifies range and nature of evidence without deep quality assessment

Asks: how much research exists? What types of studies? What key concepts emerge? Maps the terrain of a field rather than synthesizing its conclusions.

Best for: Emerging or complex topics where you want to establish what’s out there before committing to a narrower focus.
Output: A conceptual map of the field — useful as a precursor to a deeper, more targeted review.

Meta-analysis — Quantitative synthesis across studies

Statistically pools data from multiple independent studies to estimate overall effect

Requires quantitative data from multiple comparable studies. Produces a pooled effect size statistically more powerful than any single study alone.

Best for: Health interventions, psychological phenomena, educational outcomes — anywhere many similar studies exist.
Why it matters: Any single study can be an outlier. Meta-analysis finds the aggregate signal across the noise.

UG students encounter meta-analyses as sources — they do not typically conduct them.

Literature Review — Process

How to Conduct a Literature Review

The process is sequential. Each step builds on the one before it. Rushing any step undermines everything downstream.

  1. 1
    Define keywords and search termsIdentify core concepts, develop synonyms, and use Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT). A rigorous keyword strategy determines what you find — and what you miss.
  2. 2
    Search credible academic databasesGoogle Scholar, JSTOR, PubMed, Scopus, IEEE Xplore — depending on your field. Not Google. Not Wikipedia. Prioritize peer-reviewed journals and reputable conference papers.
  3. 3
    Screen using a three-stage funnelTitle relevance → Abstract relevance → Full text. Do not read everything in full immediately. Narrow from many results to the papers that genuinely serve your focus and scope.
  4. 4
    Read critically, not just descriptivelyFor each paper: What does it claim? What evidence? What methodology? What are its limitations? What does it leave unanswered? This analytical reading is the raw material of your review.
  5. 5
    Organize by theme — not by paperGroup sources by the arguments, patterns, or debates they reveal — not by author or date. This is the single most important structural choice that separates a review from a series of summaries.
  6. 6
    Write synthesizing paragraphs that build toward the gapEach paragraph makes an argument about what the literature shows on a theme — and contributes to establishing why a gap exists. Every paragraph moves you closer to your research justification.

Research Objectives

What they are, why they matter, and how your literature review earns them. The direct logical consequence of the work you’ve just done.

Research Objectives

What are Research Objectives and Why Do They Matter?

Definition

Research objectives are the specific, measurable, and achievable goals your research intends to accomplish — what you will do, what you will examine, and what you will be able to conclude by the end of your study.

They emerge from the gap

Your LR identifies what is missing. Your objective says: “This study will address that gap by doing X.” One cannot exist without the other — objectives that don’t stem from a gap are arbitrary.

They define and protect scope

Without clear objectives, research expands indefinitely. Objectives constrain your study to what is achievable — the most common UG research failure is uncontrolled scope.

They make evaluation possible

Without objectives, there is no criterion by which to judge whether your research succeeded. They are the standard against which findings are assessed at every stage.

The Logical Spine

The Research Chain

Every well-structured research project follows this causal sequence. Each step earns the right to the next. Skipping or rushing the literature review creates structural problems in everything that follows.

Ethics, Citations & Plagiarism

Attribution is the backbone of academic integrity. This section covers what you owe your sources — and the consequences of not paying it.

Research Ethics

Research Ethics

Research ethics are the principles that determine whether your research is worthy of trust — by your field, your institution, and anyone who might rely on your findings.

  • ✓
    Intellectual honestyReport what you found — not what you hoped to find. Never selectively omit contradictory evidence to make your argument appear stronger than it is.
  • ✓
    Proper attribution — alwaysEvery idea, claim, data point, or argument that is not originally yours must be attributed to its source, every time, without exception.
  • ✓
    No fabrication or misrepresentationInvented data, manipulated results, or misquoted sources do not just harm your work — they can corrupt research that builds on it, with serious academic and professional consequences.
  • ✓
    Respect for sources and subjectsRepresent sources accurately and fairly. If your research involves human participants, follow institutional protocols for informed consent and confidentiality.

If you fabricate data or misattribute ideas, your conclusions are worthless — not only to you, but to every researcher who might have built on your work.

Citations

What is a Citation and Why Does it Matter?

Definition

A citation is a formal acknowledgment of the source of an idea, claim, quotation, or data used in your research. It tells the reader exactly where the information came from — and enables them to locate, verify, and build on it.

Attribution

Scholarly knowledge is cumulative. Every idea you use was built by someone before you. Citation acknowledges that intellectual debt and credits those who did the work.

Reproducibility

Any reader can follow your citations to verify your sources, test your claims, or continue research from the same foundation. Without citations, none of this is possible.

Credibility

Well-cited research demonstrates that your arguments are grounded in verified scholarship — not personal opinion, hearsay, or unverifiable assertion.

Working rule: Any claim in your research that did not come from your own original data or analysis requires a citation. No claim without a source.

The Critical Distinction

Citation, Paraphrasing & Plagiarism

Direct Citation

Exact text, properly attributed

Quoting an author’s exact words, in quotation marks, with a full in-text citation. Use sparingly — only when precise wording is analytically significant and cannot be paraphrased without losing meaning.

Paraphrasing

Rewritten — still requires attribution

Restating someone else’s idea in your own words. Paraphrasing is the preferred mode in academic writing. However — paraphrasing still requires a citation. Rewriting does not make the idea yours.

Plagiarism

Any idea used without attribution

Using someone else’s idea, text, data, or structure without acknowledging the source. Defined by attribution failure, not surface-level copying. Paraphrasing without a citation is plagiarism.

The Most Common Misconception

“I changed the words, so it’s not plagiarism.” — Incorrect. If you took the idea from a source and did not cite that source, it is plagiarism regardless of how thoroughly you rewrote the sentence.

Citation Styles

APA — American Psychological Association (7th ed.)

Standard in social sciences, psychology, education, and STEM. Format: (Author, Year, p. #) in-text; full entry in the Reference List.

In-text citation — within a literature review paragraph Research on digital distraction has demonstrated significant negative effects on reading comprehension among undergraduate students (Sharma & Patel, 2021, p. 134). This finding aligns with earlier evidence linking multitasking behaviour to measurable reductions in academic retention (Rosen, 2018).
Reference List Entry — Journal Article Sharma, R., & Patel, K. (2021). Digital distraction and comprehension in undergraduate classrooms. Journal of Educational Research, 45(3), 128–141. https://doi.org/10.xxxx/example
Reference List Entry — Book Rosen, L. D. (2018). iDisorder: Understanding our obsession with technology. Palgrave Macmillan.

APA is likely required if you are in social sciences, business, health, education, or STEM. Always verify with your department.

Citation Styles

MLA — Modern Language Association (9th ed.)

Standard in humanities — literature, language, arts, cultural studies, film. Format: (Author page#) in-text; full entry in Works Cited.

In-text citation — within a literature review paragraph Scholars of postcolonial literature argue that language functions as a primary instrument of cultural erasure, with indigenous vocabularies systematically displaced through colonial discourse (Thiong’o 9). This position is elaborated in analyses of identity formation across generational contexts (Spivak 76).
Works Cited Entry — Book Thiong’o, Ngũgĩ wa. Decolonising the Mind: The Politics of Language in African Literature. James Currey, 1986.
Works Cited Entry — Essay in an Edited Collection Spivak, Gayatri Chakravorty. “Can the Subaltern Speak?” Colonial Discourse and Post-Colonial Theory, edited by Patrick Williams and Laura Chrisman, Columbia UP, 1994, pp. 66–111.

MLA is likely required in English literature, languages, media studies, philosophy, or fine arts. Check your department handbook.

Introduction to Statistics

What is Statistics Population & Sample Parameter vs Statistic Data Types Descriptive Statistics Sampling Methods Hypothesis Testing
Definition

What is Statistics?

Definition

Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing, and interpreting data to assist in evidence-based decision-making.

Collection

Gathering information through surveys, experiments, observations, and other data-collection methods.

Organization

Arranging data in tables, classifications, and frequency distributions to make it manageable and interpretable.

Presentation

Communicating data visually through graphs, charts, histograms, and diagrams for clearer understanding.

Analysis

Calculating measures such as mean, median, standard deviation, and correlation to extract meaning from raw data.

Interpretation & Inference

Drawing conclusions about a population based on sample information — the ultimate purpose of statistical work.

Example: A college has 2,000 students. The Principal wants to know the average daily study hours. Studying all 2,000 is impractical — so 100 students are selected, their data is collected, analyzed, and used to draw conclusions about all 2,000.

Core Concepts

Population and Sample

Population

The complete set

A population is the complete set of individuals, objects, or observations about which the researcher wants to draw a conclusion. It is the entire group of interest.

Examples:
All 2,000 students in a college
All households in Maharashtra
All employees of a company

Sample

A selected subset

A sample is a smaller group selected from the population for the purpose of conducting a study. It must represent the population from which it is drawn.

Example:
College has 2,000 students.
Population = 2,000 students.
Sample = 200 students selected for a survey.

Findings from 200 are used to make inferences about all 2,000.

Key Numerical Measures

Population Parameter and Sample Statistic

Population Parameter

A numerical measure from the entire population

Calculated using data from every member of the population. Often represented by Greek letters.

Example: Average marks of all 2,000 students = 65
Population mean — μ = 65

Sample Statistic

A numerical measure from a selected sample

Calculated using data from only the selected sample members. Used to estimate the population parameter.

Example: Average marks of 200 selected students = 63
Sample mean — x̄ = 63

Why use sample data instead of studying the entire population?

Investigating the full population is time-consuming, cost-intensive, and manpower-demanding. A well-chosen sample provides reliable estimates of population parameters at a fraction of the effort — which is why sampling methods matter.

Types of Data

Primary and Secondary Data

Primary Data

Collected directly by the researcher

Primary data is gathered first-hand for the specific purpose of the study. It is original, current, and directly relevant to the research question.

Methods:

Questionnaire • Personal interview • Observation • Experiment • Survey • Focus group

Example: A researcher prepares a questionnaire on social media usage and collects responses directly from 300 students.

Secondary Data

Already collected by someone else

Secondary data has been collected by another person, organization, or institution and is subsequently used by the researcher for a different or related purpose.

Sources:

Census reports • Government publications • Research papers • Books & journals • Company annual reports • Institutional records • Websites & databases

Example: To study India’s population growth, a researcher uses Census reports instead of conducting a new survey.

Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive Statistics — Measures of Central Tendency

What is Descriptive Statistics?

Descriptive Statistics refers to methods used to organize, summarize, and present data in a meaningful form. It describes the data we have, without drawing broader conclusions beyond what was directly observed.

Measures of Central Tendency describe the central or typical value of a dataset.

Mean

Arithmetic average

Sum of all observations divided by the total number of observations. The most commonly used measure of center.

FormulaMean = ΣXᵢ / n
Median

The middle value

The middle value when observations are arranged in ascending or descending order. Not affected by extreme values (outliers).

Mode

The most frequent value

The value that occurs most frequently in a dataset. A dataset can have more than one mode, or no mode at all.

Example: Marks of five students: 40, 50, 60, 70, 80
Mean = (40+50+60+70+80) / 5 = 60  •  Median = 60  •  Mode = none (all values appear once)

Descriptive Statistics

Measures of Dispersion & Data Presentation

Central tendency alone is incomplete. Dispersion tells us how widely spread the observations are around that center.

Measures of Dispersion

Range • Variance • Standard Deviation • Interquartile Range • Coefficient of Variation

Why Dispersion Matters

Both classes have a mean of 60:

Class A: 58, 59, 60, 61, 62 — low variability
Class B: 30, 45, 60, 75, 90 — high variability

Same mean, very different distributions. Mean alone does not give the complete picture. Dispersion completes it.

Presentation of Data

Making data visible and communicable

Descriptive statistics also encompasses the visual communication of data:

  • ■  Frequency tables
  • ■  Bar charts
  • ■  Pie charts
  • ■  Histograms
  • ■  Line graphs

Descriptive statistics describes the data we have.
Inferential statistics helps us draw conclusions beyond it.

Sampling

Sampling — Definition and Importance

Definition

Sampling is the process of selecting a smaller, representative group from a population for the purpose of statistical study. The findings from the sample are then used to make inferences about the entire population.

Population: 10,000 college students → Sample: 500 students selected using an appropriate method

  • ✓
    Saves timeStudying 500 students is faster than studying 10,000. Time is a finite resource in any research project.
  • ✓
    Reduces costFewer respondents require fewer financial resources — a critical constraint in most research contexts.
  • ✓
    Easier to manageData collection, processing, and analysis become operationally feasible at sample size versus population size.
  • ✓
    Useful for large populationsSometimes studying the entire population is practically impossible — sampling makes the study feasible.
  • ✓
    Can produce reliable resultsIf the sample is properly selected and representative, it provides accurate information about the population.
The key condition

A sample is only useful if it is representative of the population. A biased sample produces biased conclusions — which is why sampling methods matter, not just sampling size.

Sampling Methods

Probability Sampling Methods

In probability sampling, every unit in the population has a known, non-zero probability of being selected — which makes the sample statistically defensible and generalizable.

Simple Random Sampling

Every member has an equal, independent chance of selection

Each population unit has the same probability of being chosen. Selection is free of any pattern or preference — purely random.

How: Use a random number generator, lottery method, or random number tables.

Example: A college has 1,000 students. The researcher randomly selects 100 using a random-number generator — every student had an equal 1-in-10 chance of selection.

Best for: Homogeneous populations where no specific sub-groups need to be represented separately.

Systematic Sampling

Every kth member selected after a random starting point

The researcher determines a sampling interval (k = population size ÷ required sample size), chooses a random starting point, then selects every kth unit thereafter.

Example: Population = 1,000 students. Required sample = 100. Sampling interval k = 1,000 ÷ 100 = 10. After a random start, every 10th student is selected.

Best for: Populations with a natural ordering (lists, registers, databases). Faster to execute than simple random sampling.

Stratified Sampling

Population divided into homogeneous strata; sample drawn from each

The population is divided into relatively homogeneous groups called strata based on a relevant characteristic, and samples are then drawn from each stratum.

Example: Students are divided by year of study — First Year, Second Year, Third Year. Students are then selected proportionally from each group.

Best for: Heterogeneous populations where important sub-groups must be represented. Produces more precise estimates than simple random sampling when strata differ from each other.

Cluster Sampling

Natural groups (clusters) identified; some clusters selected; all members within studied

The population is divided into natural groups called clusters. Some clusters are randomly selected, and all or most members within those clusters are studied.

Example: To study college students across a city, 10 colleges are randomly selected. All students within those colleges are surveyed. The colleges are the clusters.

Best for: Geographically dispersed populations where it is impractical to travel to each individual member. More economical than other methods at scale.

Hypothesis Testing

The Concept of Hypothesis Testing

What is a Statistical Hypothesis?

A statistical hypothesis is a claim or statement about a population parameter that can be evaluated using sample data. We cannot examine the entire population — so we use sample evidence to decide whether the claim is plausible.

Example: A college claims “The average marks of our students are 60.” We take a sample and ask: Is the sample evidence strong enough to question or support this claim?

Null Hypothesis — H₀

The default claim being tested

Generally represents: no difference, no effect, no relationship, or a specified population value. It is the claim we test against.

Example: H₀: μ = 60
(The average marks are 60 as claimed.)

Alternative Hypothesis — H₁

The claim that contradicts H₀

Represents what we believe may be true if H₀ is rejected.

Two-tailed: H₁: μ ≠ 60 (different in either direction)
One-tailed: H₁: μ > 60 or H₁: μ < 60

(The actual average differs from the claimed 60.)

Hypothesis Testing

Level of Significance & The Decision Rule

Level of Significance — α

The most commonly used significance level is α = 0.05. This represents the maximum acceptable probability of incorrectly rejecting a true null hypothesis (Type I error — a false positive). The researcher sets this before conducting the test.

After setting α, a test statistic is calculated from sample data (e.g. t-test, z-test, chi-square — depending on the question and data type). The resulting p-value is then compared to α to reach a decision.

Decision Rule

ConditionDecision
p > 0.05Do not reject H₀ — insufficient evidence to overturn the claim
p < 0.05Reject H₀ — sample evidence is strong enough to contradict the claim

Example: H₀: μ = 60  •  H₁: μ ≠ 60
Run test → get p-value → if p < 0.05, reject H₀ — evidence suggests the average marks differ significantly from 60.

Key Concepts

Population = entire group of interest
Sample = selected representative subset
Parameter (μ) = measure from population
Statistic (x̄) = measure from sample
Primary data = collected first-hand
Secondary data = already available
Descriptive stats = summarizes observed data
Sampling = selecting representative units from a population
Hypothesis testing = evaluates population claims using sample evidence

Practice Scenario

A college has 5,000 students. You want to study their average daily mobile-phone usage.

How would you —
• Select the sample? (Which method? Why?)
• Collect the data? (Primary or secondary?)
• Summarize it? (Which measures?)
• Test whether average usage exceeds 4 hours? (State H₀ and H₁)
